# Do Americans Really Want Urban Sprawl?

**By Sarah Wesseler, Yale Climate Connections** — **January 29, 2025**

Although sprawling development remains visible across the U.S., many urban-planning experts believe this pattern results from a dysfunctional market rather than genuine American preference. Demand exists for denser, more walkable communities, but insufficient supply constrains options.

## How Single-Family Zoning Limits Housing Choices

Jonathan Levine, professor of urban and regional planning at the University of Michigan, challenges the assumption that sprawl reflects consumer desire: "if it were the case that all Americans wanted big houses on large lots ... would we need single-family zoning? Absolutely not."

In many cities, regulations prohibit townhouses, duplexes, and apartment buildings on approximately 75% of residential land; some areas exceed 90%. Levine argues these exclusionary regulations indicate "the desire of people ... to live closer in, accepting higher densities." Planners and economists increasingly view zoning as "an impediment to the market providing" desired housing types.

## Survey Results Show Mixed Signals

The National Association of Realtors conducts biennial surveys of 2,000 residents across the nation's 50 largest metros. "The desire for walkability has been increasing steadily, really, since 2015."

2023 NAR findings:
- 56% would accept smaller yards for walkable neighborhoods
- 53% chose town houses/apartments with shorter commutes over car-dependent houses

However, a Pew Research Center survey (2023) found 53% of 5,079 respondents preferred larger, farther-apart houses with distant amenities.

## Market Prices Reveal True Demand

Michael Rodriguez, research director at Smart Growth America, argues price data indicates genuine preferences better than abstract surveys. His 2023 report on the 35 largest metros found:

- Walkable neighborhoods comprise only 1.2% of land
- Just 11.6% of non-rental housing occupies walkable areas
- Car-dependent suburbs occupy approximately 90% of land
- Walkable areas commanded 34% price premiums on average; office rents 44% higher; multifamily housing 41% higher

This "premium for walkable urbanism is a market indicator that tells us a lot of people really want this thing, but there's not enough."

## Making Suburbs Walkable

Tysons, Virginia demonstrates transformation potential — recent investment created a diverse, mixed-use center with apartments, condos, performing-arts venues, and transit connections. Rodriguez notes this shows "people want this form of living" in suburban areas, not merely downtown centers.
